# Study Guide — Life in the UK Test

Built from **408 questions across 17 practice exams** on lifeintheuktestweb.co.uk, cross-referenced against the "Top 15 most common topics" YouTube revision video.

Companion file: `life_in_uk_questions.csv` (every question, answer, theme tag and difficulty rating).

## ⚠️ Read this first: the video vs. the actual question bank

I checked all 15 of the video's topics against all 408 questions. The video is a decent *starter*, but on this evidence it is **not sufficient on its own**, and a couple of its claims need correcting.

### 1. One outright factual error in the video

> The video says: *"The battle of Waterloo was **also a naval battle** where Napoleon was defeated."*

**This is wrong.** Waterloo (1815) was a **land** battle — Napoleon was defeated by the **Duke of Wellington**. The bank's own explanation confirms it: *"the French Wars ended with the defeat of the Emperor Napoleon by the Duke of Wellington at the Battle of Waterloo."*

**Trafalgar** is the naval one (Admiral Nelson, who died in the battle). The video gets Trafalgar right, then mislabels Waterloo the same way. Since the bank tests both — and tests Nelson/Trafalgar **4 times** — this is a mistake worth un-learning.

*(Also, the auto-transcript renders the first Union flag year as "6006" — that should read **1606**.)*

In 408 questions, **prizes come up twice**, and four of the six prizes it teaches **never appear at all**. Same story with the Union Flag: several minutes on the heraldry of the three crosses, but the bank only ever asks the one-line version.

The devolved-administration **member counts** are worth a caveat too: the bank tests **Wales = 60** and **Northern Ireland = 90**, but **never asks the Scottish 129**. Learn all three anyway (it's cheap), just don't assume the video's list is the tested list.

### 3. What the video gets right — genuinely high-yield

These are real, and worth front-loading:

- **Holidays & festivals — 29 questions (7.1%)**, the single biggest topic it names.
- **Women's suffrage — 19 questions.** The video's date breakdown is exactly right and the distinction matters: the bank asks *"what year were women given the right to vote?"* → **1918** (over 30), **and** *"when did women get the vote at the same age as men?"* → **1928** (at 21). Emmeline Pankhurst is tested 3×.
- **Key battles — 16 questions.** Hastings, Bosworth, Trafalgar, Waterloo, Britain all appear.
- **Patron saints — 14 questions.** All four saints *and* all four dates are tested. Pure marks.
- **Elizabeth I / Spanish Armada — 14 questions.**
- **Sport — 14 questions.** Ashes, Six Nations, Grand National, Royal Ascot, Olympics (UK hosted **3** times) all confirmed.
- **Legal minimum ages — 12 questions.**

### 4. The big one: what the video never mentions

**254 of 408 questions (62%) are not touched by any of the video's 15 topics.** And the untouched material is *harder* on average — **5.4/10 vs 4.9/10** for the topics the video does cover.

The largest blind spots, by question count:

The pattern: the video covers the **list-shaped, quiz-friendly** topics (flowers, foods, saints, flags, prizes) and skips the **institutional and procedural** material — how Parliament works, how courts and police work, how elections run, how tax and public services work. That institutional block alone is **~70 questions**, roughly **five times** the weight of every topic the video opens with.

**Bottom line:** use the video for the memorisation lists, then spend most of your revision time on Government, Law, and the History periods below — that's where the marks and the difficulty actually are.

## Known flaws in the source site (don't learn these wrong)

While building this guide I found a number of places where the site's own answer or explanation is sloppy or self-contradictory. The **keyed answer is what the site marks correct**, but the reasoning attached to it is sometimes wrong:

- **Channel Islands (Exam 1 Q20)** — keys "Crown dependency", then the explanation calls them "a British overseas territory". The explanation is the wrong part. Two other questions (Exam 12 Q1, Exam 17 Q6) key it correctly: Channel Islands and Isle of Man = **Crown dependencies**; St Helena and the Falklands = **overseas territories**.
- **Nelson at Trafalgar (Exam 9 Q15)** — explanation says "against the Spanish fleet". It was the **combined French and Spanish** fleet.
- **Charles II "defeated by Cromwell" (Exam 5 Q10)** — calls him "king" at a time when he had not yet been crowned (he was restored in 1660).
- **Netherlands monarch (Exam 16 Q7)** — keys **Charles II** (returning from exile), which collides with other questions where **William of Orange** comes from the Netherlands to take the throne. Ambiguous as worded; read the options carefully.
- **Tribes after the Romans (Exam 16 Q4)** — keys "the Jutes" while its own explanation lists Jutes, Angles *and* Saxons. Only the option list disambiguates it.
- **BBC (Exam 12 Q2)** — explanation calls the BBC "state-funded" *and* "independent of government". It is **licence-fee funded**.
- **Welsh "government" (Exams 8 Q20, 10 Q10)** and **"Northern Ireland Parliament" (Exam 14 Q2)** — the site conflates the *government* with the *legislature*. The 60 members belong to the **Senedd**; Northern Ireland has an **Assembly**, not a Parliament.
- **Emily Watson (Exam 14 Q4)** — "has not won an Oscar **yet**" is a time-sensitive fact that could go stale.
- **1918 vs 1928 (Exam 8 Q16 vs Exams 1/4/9)** — not an error but the bank's single nastiest trap. "*What year were women given the right to vote?*" → **1918** (women over 30). "*When did women get the vote at the same age as men?*" → **1928** (at 21). Read the wording before answering.

# Study notes by area

## History

*150 questions across 16 themes, overall avg difficulty 5.4. `⚠️` = the bank rates this question 7+ out of 10 (high error rate). `(tested Nx)` = the same fact is asked N different ways.*

### Norman Conquest & the Middle Ages
**21 questions | avg difficulty 5.1**

- **1066 — the Norman Conquest was the LAST successful foreign invasion of England** (tested 2x)
- Battle of Hastings — William the Conqueror defeated **Harold, the Saxon king**; William was **Duke of Normandy (now northern France)**; the battle is commemorated in the **Bayeux Tapestry** (tested 2x)
- ⚠️ William the Conqueror built the **Tower of London** — the **White Tower** is an example of a Norman castle keep (tested 2x)
- The **Domesday Book** — William sent people all over England to list towns, villages, the people living there, who owned the land and what animals they owned; written **after the Norman Conquest**; still exists and shows society just after 1066 (tested 4x)
- The two records that tell us about England under **William I** = the **Domesday Book** and the **Bayeux Tapestry**
- ⚠️ After the Conquest the king and noblemen spoke **Norman French**, the peasants spoke **Anglo-Saxon**; the two combined into one English language (tested 2x)
- The **Black Death** came to Britain in **1348** — probably a form of **plague**; **one third** of the population of England died, and a similar proportion in Scotland and Wales (tested 4x)
- The **Hundred Years War** — English kings' long war with **France**; despite the name it actually lasted **116 years**
- **1314, Battle of Bannockburn** — the Scots, led by **Robert the Bruce**, defeated the English; Scotland remained unconquered (tested 2x)
- **Serfs** = peasants who had a small area of their lord's agricultural land where they could grow food
- ⚠️ **William Caxton** — the first person in England to print books using a printing press

### Tudors & the Reformation
**18 questions | avg difficulty 4.8**

- ⚠️ **1588 — the English defeated the Spanish Armada**, a large fleet of ships sent by **Spain** to conquer England and restore Catholicism (tested 5x — the single highest-yield fact in the bank)
- **Henry VIII established the Church of England** because the **Pope refused** to let him divorce his first wife, **Catherine of Aragon** (tested 2x)
- The **Reformation** = the movement against the authority of the Pope and the ideas and practices of the Roman Catholic Church, in Henry VIII's reign (tested 3x)
- **Anne Boleyn**, wife of Henry VIII, was unpopular and accused of taking lovers — executed at the **Tower of London**
- **Elizabeth I** — daughter of **Henry VIII**; a **Protestant**; she found a balance between the views of the Catholics and the more extreme Protestants; one of the most popular monarchs in English history, especially after 1588 (tested 3x)
- ⚠️ **Mary Stuart ('Mary, Queen of Scots') was a Catholic**; imprisoned for **20 years** by her cousin Elizabeth I, then **executed**, accused of plotting against Elizabeth (tested 3x)
- **1560 — the Scottish Parliament** abolished the Pope's authority in Scotland and made Roman Catholic services illegal; a **Protestant Church of Scotland** with an **elected leadership** was established — but, unlike England, **not a state Church**

### Empire, exploration, slavery & abolition
**17 questions | avg difficulty 5.4**

- **Battle of Waterloo, 1815** — the **last** battle between Britain and **France**; the **Duke of Wellington** defeated **Emperor Napoleon**, ending the French Wars (tested 2x)
- **Battle of Trafalgar** — **Admiral Nelson** commanded the British fleet and was **killed** in the battle (tested 2x here, 2x more under Notable Britons)
- The **Boer War, 1899–1902**, took place in **South Africa** against the **Boers** — settlers from the **Netherlands**; they fought fiercely and the war lasted over three years (tested 3x)
- ⚠️ The **Crimean War, 1853–56** — Britain fought **with Turkey and France against Russia**; it was the **first war extensively covered by the media** (news stories and photographs) (tested 2x)
- ⚠️ **1833 — the Emancipation Act abolished slavery throughout the British Empire** (tested 2x)
- **William Wilberforce** — an evangelical Christian and **Member of Parliament** (keyed answer: "a politician"); with other abolitionists he turned public opinion against the slave trade
- The American colonies wanted independence because **the British government wanted to tax them**; in **1776, 13 North American colonies** declared independence, demanding **'no taxation without representation'** and stating people had a right to establish their own governments (tested 2x)
- ⚠️ English settlers first began to colonise the **eastern coast of America** in the reign of **Elizabeth I**
- **Captain James Cook** mapped the coast of **Australia**; a few colonies were established there
- During the **18th century**, overseas trading and settlements brought Britain into conflict particularly with **France**

### Stuarts, Civil War & Restoration
**16 questions | avg difficulty 6.1**

- The **English Civil War began in 1642** — the country split into the **Cavaliers** (supported the king) and the **Roundheads** (supported Parliament) (tested 2x)
- **Charles I** believed in the **'Divine Right of Kings'** (the king appointed directly by God) and tried to **rule without Parliament**; ⚠️ his army was defeated at the **Battles of Marston Moor and Naseby**; held prisoner, he refused agreement and was **executed in 1649** (tested 4x)
- When Charles I was executed, **England declared itself a republic called the Commonwealth** — it no longer had a monarch
- **Oliver Cromwell** was given the title **Lord Protector** and ruled until his death in **1658**
- ⚠️ **Charles II** — defeated by Cromwell, famously **hid in an oak tree** escaping from Worcester and fled to Europe; in **May 1660** Parliament invited him back from exile **in the Netherlands** and he was crowned King of England, Wales, Scotland and Ireland (tested 3x)
- During **Charles II's reign** a **great fire** destroyed much of London, including many churches and **St Paul's Cathedral**
- ⚠️ The **Habeas Corpus Act** became law in **1679** — Latin for *'you must present the person in court'*; no one can be held prisoner unlawfully and **every prisoner has a right to a court hearing**
- The **Huguenots** — refugees who came to England from **France** between **1680 and 1720**
- **James I** was **James VI of Scotland** before becoming King James I of England, Wales and Ireland when Elizabeth I died; Scotland remained a separate country
- ⚠️ **1745 — Charles Edward Stuart ('Bonnie Prince Charlie')**, grandson of **James II**, landed in Scotland, was supported by **Highland clansmen** and raised an army, trying to put a Stuart king back on the throne in place of George I's son, **George II**

### Industrial Revolution & invention
**15 questions | avg difficulty 5.5**

- The **Industrial Revolution** = the rapid development of industry in Britain in the **18th and 19th centuries**; Britain was the **first country to industrialise on a large scale**, thanks to machinery and **steam power** (tested 2x)
- **Manufacturing** became the main source of employment in Britain during the **18th century**
- **Canals** were built to link the factories to **towns, cities and the ports**, particularly in the new industrial areas of the middle and north of England
- **Isambard Kingdom Brunel** — engineer from Portsmouth who built tunnels, bridges, railway lines and ships; constructed the **Great Western Railway**, the first major railway in Britain, running from **Paddington Station** to south-west England
- The **Great Exhibition of 1851** was held in **Hyde Park** in the **Crystal Palace**, a huge building made of **steel and glass**
- ⚠️ Victorian **free trade** was promoted by **abolishing a number of taxes on imported goods** — e.g. the **repeal of the Corn Laws in 1846**, which had prevented the import of cheap grain
- ⚠️ The **Factories Act of 1847** limited the hours **women and children** could work to **10 hours per day**
- ⚠️ The **Highland Clearances** — Scottish landlords destroyed small farms (**'crofts'**) to make space for large flocks of **sheep and cattle**
- In the middle of the century the **potato** crop failed and Ireland suffered a **famine** — **a million people died** from disease and starvation
- ⚠️ The **Fenians** — 19th-century Irish nationalists who favoured **complete independence** from the UK
- **John Logie Baird**, a **Scotsman**, developed the **television** in the **1920s**
- **Sir Frank Whittle** developed the **jet engine** in Britain in the **1930s**
- **Concorde**, the world's only supersonic commercial airliner, was developed by **Britain and France** (tested 2x across themes)
- True/false trap: *"The last battle between Britain and France was the Battle of Waterloo"* = **True**

### Ancient, Roman & early Britain
**12 questions | avg difficulty 6.4**

- The first people to live in Britain, in the **Stone Age**, were **hunter-gatherers**
- ⚠️ The first **farmers** arrived in Britain **6,000 years ago**; their ancestors probably came from **south-east Europe** (tested 2x — rated 9/10, the hardest in the bank)
- ⚠️ The people of the **Iron Age** made the **first coins minted in Britain**, some inscribed with the **names of Iron Age kings** — this marks the beginnings of British history (tested 3x)
- **Julius Caesar** led the first Roman invasion in **55 BC** — unsuccessful; Britain stayed separate from the Roman Empire for nearly 100 years until **AD 43**, when **Emperor Claudius** led a new invasion
- **Boudicca**, queen of the **Iceni** in what is now **eastern England**, was a tribal leader who fought against the **Romans** (tested 3x)
- ⚠️ **Emperor Hadrian** built a wall in the **north of England** to keep out the **Picts** (ancestors of the Scottish people); its forts include **Housesteads and Vindolanda** (tested 2x)

### First & Second World Wars
**10 questions | avg difficulty 3.8**

- **28 June 1914** — the assassination of **Archduke Franz Ferdinand of Austria** set off the chain of events leading to the **First World War (1914–18)**
- The **First World War ended at 11.00 am on 11 November 1918**, with victory for Britain and its allies (tested 2x)
- The **Second World War** started when **Hitler/Germany invaded Poland in 1939**; Britain and France declared war to stop his aggression (tested 3x)
- The **Battle of Britain**, summer **1940**, was a crucial **aerial** battle between Germany and Britain, fought by the **Royal Air Force** (tested 3x)
- The **'Dunkirk spirit'** — as France fell, Britain evacuated British and French soldiers in a huge naval operation; civilian volunteers in small pleasure and fishing boats helped the Navy rescue **more than 300,000 men** from the beaches around **Dunkirk**

### Suffrage & women's rights (Suffragettes)
**10 questions | avg difficulty 6.0**

- ⚠️ **1918 — women over the age of 30** were given voting rights and the right to **stand for Parliament**, partly in recognition of women's contribution to the war effort during the **First World War** (tested 3x)
- ⚠️ **1928 — women were given the right to vote at 21, the same as men** (tested 3x)
- ⚠️ Trap: a bare *"What year were women given the right to vote?"* is keyed **1918**; *"at the same age as men / at 21"* is keyed **1928**
- The **suffragettes** — in the late 19th and early 20th centuries women campaigned and demonstrated for greater rights, especially the vote, forming the **women's suffrage movement**
- **Emmeline Pankhurst** — born in **Manchester in 1858**; leader of the suffragettes; set up the **Women's Franchise League in 1889** (the vote in local elections for married women); helped found the **Women's Social and Political Union (WSPU) in 1903**, the first group whose members were called 'suffragettes'; used civil disobedience (tested 3x)

### 20th-century & post-war Britain
**8 questions | avg difficulty 5.8**

- ⚠️ In **1913** the British government promised **'Home Rule'** for Ireland, but changes were delayed until **1921** because of the **outbreak of the First World War**
- ⚠️ **Ireland became a republic in 1949**
- ⚠️ During the **Great Depression** of the **1930s**, traditional **heavy industries such as shipbuilding** were badly affected
- ⚠️ The **Education Act 1944 ('the Butler Act')** introduced **free secondary education in England and Wales**; **Richard Austen Butler** became a Conservative MP in 1923 and was made responsible for education in 1941
- The **1960s** = the **'swinging sixties'** — significant social change, growth in British **fashion, cinema and popular music** (**The Beatles**, **The Rolling Stones**), and **social laws were liberalised** (tested 2x)
- **Concorde**, the world's only supersonic commercial airliner, was developed by Britain and **France**
- The **United Nations** was set up **after the Second World War**, to prevent war and promote international peace and security

### Glorious Revolution & Bill of Rights
**8 questions | avg difficulty 5.8**

- **1688** — important **Protestants** in England asked **William of Orange** to invade England and proclaim himself king; there was **no resistance** and he took the throne — later called the **'Glorious Revolution'** (tested 2x)
- ⚠️ **Battle of the Boyne, Ireland, 1690** — **William of Orange defeated James II**
- ⚠️ The **Bill of Rights, 1689**, confirmed **the rights of Parliament and the limits of the king's power** — it did **NOT** give the vote to all adult men, and did **NOT** give women the vote (both keyed **False**) (tested 2x)
- The laws passed after the Glorious Revolution marked the beginning of the **constitutional monarchy**
- ⚠️ The **MacDonalds of Glencoe** — the Scottish clan killed for not taking the oath
- **Bonnie Prince Charlie** was supported by clansmen from the Scottish **Highlands** (tested 2x across themes)

### Enlightenment, science & discovery
**6 questions | avg difficulty 4.0**

- The **Enlightenment** = the **18th-century** period when new ideas about **politics, philosophy and science** were developed (tested 2x)
- **Isaac Newton** — discovered **gravity**; his most famous work, *Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica* ('Mathematical Principle of Natural Philosophy'), showed how gravity applied to the whole universe; he also discovered that **white light is made up of the colours of the rainbow** (tested 2x)
- **Adam Smith** developed ideas about **economics** that are still referred to today
- **David Hume** — a famous **philosopher** who developed ideas about human nature in the 18th century

### Anglo-Saxons & Vikings
**3 questions | avg difficulty 6.7**

- ⚠️ The Anglo-Saxon kingdoms in England united under **King Alfred the Great**, who **defeated the Vikings** (tested 2x)
- ⚠️ After the Romans left in **AD 410**, Britain was invaded by tribes from northern Europe — the **Jutes**, the **Angles** and the **Saxons** (the keyed answer to the exam question is **the Jutes**)

### Magna Carta & early rights
**2 questions | avg difficulty 5.0**

- The **Magna Carta** was created in **1215**
- It **restricted the King's power** — establishing that even the king was subject to the law, protecting the rights of the **nobility**, and restricting the king's power to collect taxes or to make and change laws; in future the king would need to involve his noblemen in decisions

### Notable Britons & achievements
**2 questions | avg difficulty 5.0**

- **Admiral Nelson** was the British officer **in charge of the British fleet at the Battle of Trafalgar**, and was **killed** in the battle (tested 2x here, 2x more under Empire)
- The bank describes Trafalgar as fought **against the Spanish fleet** *(as keyed by the site)*

### Wars of the Roses
**1 question | avg difficulty 5.0**

- The **Wars of the Roses** were fought between the supporters of two families: the **House of Lancaster** and the **House of York**

### Festivals, holidays & traditions
**27 questions | avg difficulty 3.7**

- Bank holidays = public holidays each year when banks and many other businesses are closed for the day (tested 3x)
- Christmas Day = **25 December**; celebrates the birth of Jesus Christ (tested 2x)
- Boxing Day = **26 December**, the day after Christmas Day, and a public holiday
- Easter takes place in **March or April**
- Good Friday = the day Jesus Christ **died**; Easter Sunday = his rising from the dead (tested 2x)
- Lent = the **40 days before Easter** (tested 2x)
- Halloween = **31 October**
- April Fool's Day = **1 April**; people play jokes on each other until **midday**, and TV/newspapers run joke stories (tested 2x)
- Hogmanay = **New Year's Eve, 31 December**, as it is called in Scotland (tested 3x)
- **Auld Lang Syne** is the song sung at New Year / Hogmanay in the UK and other countries
- Diwali = the **Festival of Lights**; celebrated by **Hindus and Sikhs** (tested 2x)
- Eid al-Fitr celebrates the **end of Ramadan**, when Muslims have fasted for a month
- St Patrick's Day **is** a public holiday in Northern Ireland — TRUE. Only **Scotland and Northern Ireland** have their patron saint's day as an official holiday (in Scotland not all businesses/offices close)
- ⚠️ St Andrew's Day (patron of Scotland) = **30 November**
- National flowers: **Wales = daffodil**, **Scotland = thistle**, **Northern Ireland = shamrock** (tested 3x, one question each)
- "The Fringe" (Edinburgh Festival Fringe) takes place in **Edinburgh**, Scotland, every summer — the biggest part of the Edinburgh Festival, a series of arts and cultural festivals

### Religion & patron saints
**16 questions | avg difficulty 5.4**

- Patron saints: **England = St George**, **Scotland = St Andrew**, **Wales = St David**, **Northern Ireland = St Patrick** (tested 4x, one question each)
- Patron saint days: ⚠️ **St David's = 1 March**, **St Patrick's = 17 March**, **St George's = 23 April** (and St Andrew's = 30 November, asked under Festivals)
- ⚠️ There is **no established Church in Wales or Northern Ireland** — both "Wales has its own established church" and "Northern Ireland has its own established church" are **FALSE** (tested 3x)
- ⚠️ The Church of Scotland is the national Church of Scotland and is **Presbyterian**
- The **monarch** is the head of the Church of England
- The **Archbishop of Canterbury** is the spiritual leader of the Church of England
- ⚠️ **St Augustine** was the **first Archbishop of Canterbury**; he led missionaries from Rome who spread Christianity in the south
- King James I's new English translation of the Bible = the **"Authorised Version"** (also called the King James Version)
- The **Puritans** were a group of **Protestants** who advocated strict and simple religious doctrine and worship

### Food, leisure & everyday life
**13 questions | avg difficulty 4.0**

- Traditional foods: **haggis = Scotland**; **Ulster fry = Northern Ireland**
- Minimum ages — **18** to buy alcohol (criminal offence to sell to or buy for under-18s); exception: **16 or over may drink alcohol with a meal in a hotel or restaurant**
- Minimum ages — **18** to enter betting shops or gambling clubs
- Minimum ages — **17** to drive a car or a motorcycle; **16** to ride a moped; special tests/age rules apply to large vehicles
- You must have a **valid driving licence** to drive on public roads in the UK
- Dogs in public places must wear a **collar showing the owner's name AND address** (tested 2x)
- The **National Trust** = charity that preserves important **buildings, coastline and countryside** in the UK
- **Friends of the Earth** = an **environmental** charity (tested 2x)
- Most shops in the UK are **open seven days a week**, though trading hours on Sundays and public holidays are generally reduced

### Literature & writers
**13 questions | avg difficulty 4.2**

- **William Shakespeare** was born in **Stratford-upon-Avon**, England (tested 2x)
- Shakespeare plays named in the bank: **A Midsummer Night's Dream** and **Macbeth** (tested 2x)
- **Geoffrey Chaucer** wrote **The Canterbury Tales** — a collection of **poems** (not novels/plays) about a group of people going to Canterbury on a **pilgrimage**, telling each other stories on the journey (tested 3x)
- **William Wordsworth** wrote **'The Daffodils'**; he is the poet "inspired by nature" (tested 3x)
- **Sir Arthur Conan Doyle** wrote **Sherlock Holmes**
- **The Lord of the Rings** by **JRR Tolkien** was voted the country's best-loved novel in **2003**
- The **Man Booker Prize** is awarded in **literature** — annually for the best fiction novel by an author from the **Commonwealth, Ireland or Zimbabwe**; awarded **since 1968**; past winners include **Ian McEwan, Hilary Mantel, Julian Barnes**

### Money, work, tax & public services

### Parliament & how government works
**23 questions | avg difficulty 5.0**

- The UK Parliament is formed of two houses: the **House of Commons** and the **House of Lords**
- Debates in the House of Commons are chaired by **the Speaker**
- The Speaker is an MP, represents a constituency and deals with constituents — true
- The **Home Secretary** is responsible for **crime, policing and immigration** (tested 3x)
- Several **Church of England bishops** sit in the House of Lords — true
- Members of the House of Lords, known as **peers**, are **not elected** by the people

- **Prime Minister's Questions** takes place **every week** while Parliament is sitting
- MPs' responsibilities: represent everyone in their **constituency**; help to create new laws; scrutinise and comment on what the government is doing; debate important national issues (tested 2x)
- MPs can be contacted **by letter or telephone at their constituency office, or at their office in the House of Commons** — not only by post, and not only at the Commons. Many MPs, SMs, MSPs and MEPs also hold local **'surgeries'** (tested 2x)
- Britain does **not** have a written constitution — it is not in any single document, so it is described as **'unwritten'**

- The leader of the opposition appoints senior opposition MPs as **'shadow ministers'**; together they form the **Shadow Cabinet**, whose role is to challenge the government and put forward alternative policies (tested 2x)

### The legal system & courts
**17 questions | avg difficulty 6.4**
